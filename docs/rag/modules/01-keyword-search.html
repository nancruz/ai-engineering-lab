How keyword search ranks documents

Retrieval decides what the model can know

A language model in a RAG system only answers from the passages you put in front of it. Retrieval picks those few passages out of everything you have. If the passage with the answer isn't retrieved, nothing later in the pipeline can recover it. That's why the course starts here, and why most RAG debugging starts by asking "was the right passage retrieved?"

The inverted index

Scanning every document for every query is too slow. A search engine builds an inverted index once: like the index at the back of a book, it maps each term to the documents that contain it. A query looks up its terms and only scores the documents on those lists.

Scoring with BM25, one idea at a time

Once you have candidate documents, you need to put them in order. BM25 ("Best Match 25", the 25th formula its authors tried) gives each document a score, and it does it one query term at a time. For every query term the document contains it asks two questions, multiplies the answers, and adds the results up:

score(document) = Σ over query terms of [ how valuable is this term? ] × [ how much does this document use it, measured fairly? ]

The first factor is called IDF. The second is a term-frequency part controlled by two knobs, k1 and b. We'll take them one at a time, with the Lumen documents as the example throughout.

Idea 1 · IDF: rare words are better clues

Imagine you're looking for the document about error PLAY-4012, and someone hands you two clues: "it contains the word playback" and "it contains play-4012". The first clue is almost useless: 11 of the 24 Lumen documents contain "playback". The second is excellent: only 2 do.

That's the whole idea of inverse document frequency. Count how many documents contain the term; that count is its document frequency, df. The fewer documents contain it, the more a match is worth. "Inverse" just means the weight goes down as df goes up.

Three things to notice on the curve:

  • It drops fast at first. Going from 1 to 4 documents loses more weight than going from 12 to 20. Being rare matters; the difference between "common" and "very common" matters less.
  • A word in every document gets a weight near zero. It still counts a tiny bit, never negative: that's why there's a 1 + inside the logarithm (the Lucene variant).
  • The logarithm (ln) squashes the range. Without it, a term in 1 document would be worth 16.7 and a term in 2 documents 10; with it, 2.81 and 2.30. Rare terms still lead, but a single very rare word can't drown out every other term in the query.

IDF is a property of the term across the whole collection. It's the same for every document; it says nothing yet about any particular document.

Idea 2 · Term frequency and k1: repeats help, but less and less

Now look at one document. Term frequency, tf, is how many times the term appears in it. A document that mentions "timeout" five times is probably more about timeouts than one that mentions it once. But is it five times more about timeouts? Probably not: after a few mentions, more mentions tell you little.

So BM25 doesn't use the raw count. It passes it through a curve that rises quickly and then flattens out, a saturation curve:

tf part = tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1)

One occurrence always gives exactly 1. Every extra occurrence adds less than the one before, and the total can never exceed k1 + 1. So k1 is the knob for how quickly repetition stops paying off:

  • k1 = 0: the term is either present (1) or absent (0). Counting is switched off.
  • k1 around 1.2–2 (the usual defaults; this course uses 1.5): the 2nd and 3rd mention still help, the 10th barely does.
  • large k1: close to the raw count, so a document can win by repeating a word many times.

Idea 3 · Length normalization and b: don't reward documents for being long

The Lumen documents range from 37 to 265 terms; the average (avgdl) is about 144. A long document mentions more things, so it will contain a query word several times just by being long, without being more about it. If you only counted, the long handbooks would win every search.

BM25 corrects for this by comparing each document's length |d| with the average and turning that into a length factor:

length factor = 1 − b + b × |d| / avgdl

For a document of exactly average length, the factor is 1 and nothing changes. Twice the average with b = 0.75 gives 1 − 0.75 + 0.75 × 2 = 1.75. Half the average gives 0.625. The factor then multiplies k1 in the saturation curve:

tf part = tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 × length factor)

A bigger factor makes the curve fill up more slowly, so each occurrence in a long document is worth less; a smaller one makes each occurrence in a short document worth more. b is the knob for how much length matters, from 0 to 1:

  • b = 0: the factor is always 1. Length is ignored.
  • b = 1: the factor is the full ratio. A document twice as long needs about twice the mentions to score the same.
  • b = 0.75 (the common default): mostly corrected, a bit of benefit of the doubt for long documents.

The full formula, for reference
score(d, q) = Σt ∈ q idf(t) · tf(t,d) · (k1 + 1) / ( tf(t,d) + k1 · (1 − b + b · |d| / avgdl) )
idf(t) = ln( 1 + (N − df(t) + 0.5) / (df(t) + 0.5) )

tf(t,d): occurrences of t in d · df(t): documents containing t · N: number of documents · |d|: terms in d · avgdl: average |d|. The +0.5s keep the division safe when df is 0 or N. No training and no model: just counting, and it's still hard to beat on exact terms.

The whole ranking

Now all the pieces at once, on all 24 documents. Each bar is one document's score, split into the contribution of each query term. Things to try:

  • PLAY-4012: the DRM overview slightly outscores the error-code table. Both mention the code; the DRM page is shorter. Drag b to 0 and the table wins. Length normalization helped here, and hurt there.
  • k1 = 0 makes every term count once, however often it appears. Watch the saturation curve flatten.
  • catalog timeout retries: the archived 2023 standard (3000 ms, three retries) ranks above the current one. BM25 measures word overlap, not truth or freshness. You'll fix that with metadata in Module 4.
  • how do I undo a bad playback release: the deploy pipeline page wins on "bad" and "release". The runbook says "roll back", which isn't the same word. That's the vocabulary mismatch Module 2 addresses.

Key takeaways

  • Retrieval quality caps answer quality: a passage that isn't retrieved can't be used.
  • An inverted index makes keyword search fast; BM25 ranks with rarity (IDF), saturation (k1) and length normalization (b).
  • Keyword search is excellent at exact identifiers and blind to synonyms, freshness and permissions.

Lab 1: a tokenizer and a BM25 index

Goal: search the Lumen knowledge base from your terminal with your own BM25, and explain every score.

You write: minirag/text.py → tokenize() · minirag/lexical.py → BM25Index.__init__, idf, term_scores, search

  1. Tokenize

    Implement tokenize(text): lowercase, find terms with the supplied TOKEN regex, keep compounds like play-4012 whole and add their parts (play, 4012), drop stopwords. The docstring has an example.

    $ python3 -m unittest -v tests.test_lab1_search -k Tokenize
    Hint

    re.split(r"[-_]", match) gives the parts. Build a list in order; the tests check the order.

  2. Build the inverted index

    In BM25Index.__init__, for each item tokenize item.search_text() and record: a Counter of term frequencies, the document length, the document frequency of each term (once per item), and the postings (term → list of item positions).

    $ python3 -m unittest -v tests.test_lab1_search -k BuildIndex

  3. Score

    Implement idf(term) and term_scores(query_terms, position) from the formula above. A query term that appears twice counts once. Terms absent from the item are left out of the result.

    $ python3 -m unittest -v tests.test_lab1_search -k Scoring
    Hint

    dict.fromkeys(query_terms) de-duplicates while keeping order. Compute the length-normalization factor once per item, outside the loop.

  4. Search

    Implement search(query, k, allow): collect candidate positions from the postings of the query terms, skip items where allow(item) is false, sum each candidate's term scores, sort by score (highest first) then by id, and return the top k (item, score) pairs.

    $ python3 -m unittest -v tests.test_lab1_search

  5. Run it

    Search whole documents (Module 3 introduces smaller chunks) and ask for the per-term breakdown:
